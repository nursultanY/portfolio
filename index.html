Engineering, Responsibility, and Getting Stuff Done

Editorial Journal · Written January 21, 2026 · 5 min read

A reflection on how mechanical engineering can create practical impact through industry, responsibility, and disciplined execution.

My mechanical engineering degree can improve social conditions in two connected ways. First, it can help me build a stable career in industry and use that income responsibly. I want to work in oil and gas, earn well through honest technical work, and direct part of that salary to reliable charitable organizations in Qatar, Palestine, and Kazakhstan. To some people, that may sound indirect, but I see it as practical impact: a degree gives me the ability to create value, and that value can then be shared with people who need support the most.

Second, I do not think the role of a mechanical engineer is socially neutral. Even if my job is not charity itself, it still affects daily life. In oil and gas, engineers help keep energy systems reliable, safe, and efficient. When equipment runs properly, plants avoid shutdowns, waste less energy, and reduce operational risks. That means more stable supply chains, safer workplaces, and stronger economic activity. So while I may not “change society” overnight, I can contribute to systems that people depend on every day.

After graduating from Hamad Bin Khalifa University, I aim to apply my knowledge in real industrial environments, ideally in a company such as SLB or Halliburton, possibly in a role related to rotating equipment or mechanical operations. I want to solve practical problems such as equipment failure, vibration issues, pump and compressor inefficiencies, maintenance delays, and preventable downtime. These are technical problems, but they also carry economic and safety consequences, which is why solving them matters.

The best platform for me would be a large oil and gas company with strong training, high technical standards, and exposure to real field challenges. Companies like SLB, Halliburton, Wood, Weatherford, or McDermott can offer that platform because they combine engineering depth, operational scale, and opportunities to grow through responsibility. They would allow me to test my skills under pressure and develop into an engineer who delivers measurable results.

As for how I will get “STUFF DONE,” my approach is simple: I set realistic goals, break them into smaller tasks, assign clear deadlines, and stay consistent until the work is complete. I do not believe in waiting for motivation. I believe in structure, discipline, and finishing what I start.

Reviewing my resume closely taught me that professionalism is not only about achievements, but also about precision. What stood out to me first was how small issues such as unnecessary words, inconsistent punctuation, repeated phrases, and extra spacing could weaken the overall impression. These details may seem minor, but they shape how a recruiter or an ATS system reads the document. What surprised me most was that a resume can contain strong experience and still lose impact if the presentation lacks consistency. That made me realize that writing a good resume is not just about listing accomplishments, but about organizing them with discipline and clarity.

Right now, I would describe my resume as strong, but still evolving. I believe it has a solid foundation because it already reflects meaningful academic, technical, and professional experiences. It shows measurable impact, a clear structure, and relevant knowledge in engineering and related work. At the same time, I do not see it as something finished forever. For every opportunity, I still refine it based on the role, the keywords, and the skills required. In that sense, the resume is strong as a base document, but it remains a living document that should grow with me.

In the short term, the best way to improve it this semester is to strengthen both the depth and variety of my experience. I should participate in more STEM-related extracurricular activities such as hackathons, technical competitions, and research opportunities. If possible, securing another internship would be especially valuable because it would add stronger industry exposure and more evidence of practical skills. I also think I should continue improving the way I describe my work by focusing more on results, outcomes, and technical contributions rather than only responsibilities.

In the long term, I believe my resume will stand out most if I build a profile with both credibility and range. That means internships at strong international companies, certifications from respected organizations, a high CGPA, and a wider range of technical skills. Over time, I also want my resume to show not only that I studied engineering, but that I consistently applied it in research, projects, and industry settings. Overall, this process taught me that a resume is more than a document. It is a reflection of how intentionally I am developing as a professional.

After taking Gretchen Rubin’s Four Tendencies quiz, I was not surprised that I received Obliger. In my own words, an Obliger is someone who responds strongly to responsibilities that involve other people. When others are depending on me, I take the work seriously and try to meet expectations. However, I can sometimes be less strict with myself when a task depends only on my own internal motivation. Looking at my behavior in team projects, I agree that this result describes me well.

One strength this tendency brings to group work is reliability. In a team setting, I usually feel a strong sense of duty toward deadlines, communication, and assigned responsibilities. I do not like being the person who slows others down or creates uncertainty in the group. Because of that, I tend to stay organized, follow through on tasks, and make sure my part is completed on time. I think this helps create trust because teammates know they can count on me when something needs to get done.

At the same time, being an Obliger can create challenges. One risk is that I may focus too much on meeting other people’s expectations, even when I should speak up earlier, question a weak plan, or set clearer boundaries. In group work, that can sometimes lead to taking on too much or saying yes too quickly in order to keep the project moving. While that can help in the short term, it may create imbalance if it becomes a habit.

A good example of this tendency appeared in my ENGL220 class. During group work, I noticed that I naturally paid close attention to deadlines, whether each person was contributing, and whether the final output would reflect well on the team. If I felt that something was unfinished, I was likely to step in, revise, or help reorganize the work because I did not want the group to submit something weak. That behavior reflected both the strength and challenge of being an Obliger: I was dependable and committed, but I also had to be careful not to carry more than my share without communicating clearly.

In future group projects, one practical step I want to take is to pair my reliability with stronger boundary-setting. I want to remain someone others can count on, but I also want to communicate expectations earlier and encourage more balanced accountability across the team. That would make me a more effective teammate, not just a dependable one.

Today’s presentation felt like an important step in my growth, and what I am most proud of is the content of our project. I believe our work stood out because it was strongly grounded in research, evidence, and statistics rather than only general ideas. We did not just identify a problem; we proposed a realistic solution to CO2 buildup in classrooms and supported it with both technical reasoning and existing work, especially through the HVAC system concept developed at Qatar University. That made me feel confident that our project was not only creative, but also practical and credible.

The most helpful feedback was the advice to shorten certain parts and remove some slides. At first, it can feel difficult to cut material that you worked hard on, but I realized that strong communication is not about saying everything. It is about choosing what matters most and presenting it clearly. That feedback helped us focus on the most valuable points of the presentation and improve the overall flow. It reminded me that clarity and structure are just as important as research depth.

Presenting to industry mentors was also motivating. Knowing that experienced professionals would be listening made me take the quality of our work more seriously. It pushed me to think beyond the classroom and ask whether our project could stand up to practical judgment. That pressure was actually helpful, because it encouraged me to prepare more carefully and aim for a higher standard. It also made the experience feel more real, as if we were presenting ideas that could matter outside the university.

After this experience, I feel even more motivated to keep building the areas I already care about most, especially AI and oil and gas. At the same time, I now better understand the importance of combining technical skill with communication and teamwork. A good idea is not enough if it cannot be explained clearly and defended with confidence.

This presentation will help me grow as a speaker because it showed me the value of being concise, prepared, and calm under pressure. It will also help me grow as a teammate and problem-solver. I learned that choosing teammates carefully, setting expectations early, and staying punctual are all essential to producing strong work. Most importantly, I am leaving this experience with more confidence, a clearer sense of my standards, and stronger motivation to keep improving.

Throughout this course, I came to understand that technical and professional communication in the real world is not just about presenting correct information. From the guest speaker during our lecture, I learned that strong communication is about making technical ideas useful to the people who need them. In practice, engineers and professionals are not only expected to know their subject well, but also to explain it clearly, adapt it to their audience, and connect it to real decisions. That insight stayed with me because it showed me that communication is not separate from technical work; it is part of making technical work matter.

Working with my team to research, draft, and refine our technical report also deepened my understanding of how user needs shape solutions. Our project on CO2 buildup in classrooms forced us to think beyond a general problem statement and focus on what users actually experience: discomfort, reduced concentration, and an unhealthy indoor environment. It was not enough to propose an interesting idea. We had to compare alternatives carefully and justify why one solution was more practical than another. In that process, tools such as the decision matrix became especially important because they helped us evaluate solutions based on realistic criteria instead of personal preference. That experience made me appreciate how technical writing is not only about reporting results, but also about showing why a certain solution deserves to be chosen.

This course also taught me important lessons about collaboration and communication. I realized that if you take a leadership role, you must stay active and involved throughout the process. One mistake I made was assuming that everyone would approach responsibility with the same mindset and initiative that I would. Because of that, I was not fully satisfied with part of one teammate’s contribution. I learned that leadership requires more than assigning tasks; it also requires follow-up, clarity, and active communication. At the same time, I learned not to judge others too quickly based on my own standards, but instead to communicate expectations more clearly from the beginning.

Looking ahead, these skills will be very important for me as a future mechanical engineer in oil and gas. In that field, solving engineering problems is not enough on its own. I will need to communicate technical issues clearly, understand operational needs, justify solutions, and work effectively with teams under real constraints. This course strengthened my confidence in doing exactly that, and it showed me that technical communication is one of the skills that can turn engineering knowledge into real-world impact.
